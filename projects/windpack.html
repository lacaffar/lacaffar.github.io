Windpack

NC State's Collegiate Wind Competition team. I founded it in August 2025.

windpack.club ↗

Our first turbine failed inspection, and it taught me I can't build a winning one alone. So most of my job now is teaching: everyone in this room should end the year knowing more than I did when I started.

Quick summary of club

  1. Our purpose is to win this competition

    We place a 45 cm turbine in a wind tunnel, scored on power, control, safety and survival. We also produce an engineering report.

  2. Year one (2025-2026): we fell short

    We built it in one semester. The rotor shook on our only practice run, and the turbine failed tech inspection. We still placed 6th of 10 on reports and presentations. Download last year's report, PDF ↗

  3. Year two (2026-2027): we stuck together

    We thoroughly reviewed what went wrong, and before touching the turbine, we fixed how we worked as a team. We increased from 10 to 35 members, set organized deadlines with presentations to leadership, and started using metal instead of 3D prints.

Things I've personally done in Windpack

Pitch drive design

A pitch drive where one straight push turns all three blades

I designed our version of Penn State's helix drive, which sets the blade angle. The actuator only ever pushes along the shaft, and the helix turns that push into rotation.

  1. A linear actuator slides a nut along the shaft. The nut can't turn.
  2. The nut rides a two start helix, so the lead screw inside it has to turn, 72° over the full stroke.
  3. The lead screw is keyed into a 30 tooth central bevel gear.
  4. Three 20 tooth blade gears turn 1.5 times as far as the central gear. That's 108° of pitch on every blade at once, from 30 mm of stroke on the 3x model.

How I used Dynamics, Mechanics of Materials, and Multivariable Calculus

Dynamics told me where the load is. A spinning blade twists toward flat pitch with ½ J ω² sin 2θ, which peaks at 45°, right where feathering passes, so the drive is sized there. The motion study checks the geometry: 10 mm of nut travel gives 108° of pitch, in a straight line.

Mechanics of Materials checked the teeth. The Lewis equation puts 64 MPa in the tooth root at the design pitch torque. I modeled one tooth in Ansys with the real root fillet, and the peak comes out at 96 MPa. Clear V5 resin breaks at about 60, so printed teeth this size won't carry the design torque, and the final gears have to be steel. In the CAD, the tooth counts, the ratio and the cone angle are named equations.

Multivariable Calculus drew the helix. It's a space curve, r(t) = (r cos t, Lt/2π, r sin t), with the lead L set by 72° of turn over the stroke. Its tangent vector says the rib climbs at 59° to the horizontal, and its arc length gives each rib, 20.6 mm on this screw, which matches SolidWorks.

01What the competition actually scores

What I was thinking: Reliability ranks above marginal Cp gains.

The Collegiate Wind Competition puts a turbine that fits in a 45 cm cube into a wind tunnel and scores five things. Each one pushes on a different part of the machine.

TaskScoringWhat it drives
Power curve, 5 to 10 m/sPower in each bin times a weight: 0.7, 0.8, 0.8, 0.7, 0.4, 0.3. The low speed bins carry the most.Cut in torque against generator cogging; Cp at 5 to 8 m/s

SafetyShut down to about 10% RPM within 10 s on the E stop and again when the load is disconnected, then restart on your own, 50 points in four pieces.Pitch force at speed, drivetrain inertia

Any visible failure (a crack, a part coming off, sparking) stops the test, and the points stop with it. A blade that leaves its root scores nothing, however good its airfoil. So the design starts from the worst case.

02Season one, as it actually happened

What I was thinking: 6th of 10 in the least competitive region means there is real room to grow. Treat the placement as a floor, not a ceiling.

We started building late, at the start of the spring 2026 semester, as one mechanical and one electrical team. We taught ourselves QBlade and read the 2025 winners' reports. Competition was May 13 to 16 at Penn State.

What we built:

  • A18 airfoil blades, printed in PLA, which was too brittle, then ABS.
  • Each blade slid over a 6 mm shaft, held by one M3 set screw. That was the whole root.
  • Pitch by a swash plate, which was not stiff enough for three blades, then bevel gears in a 40 mm hub on a 499:1 gearmotor.
  • A TIG welded carbon steel tower, with the nacelle on a rail so parts could slide for balance.
  • A D6374 brushless motor run backwards as the generator, with a three phase short brake that needs a constant HIGH to stay off: lose the controller and the rotor stops by itself.

Then the tunnel. On our one practice slot the rotor vibrated badly on spin up, so we hit the kill switch to save the printed hub. The cause was eccentricity: printed parts that did not line up. At the event the turbine failed technical inspection, so it never ran a scored task. Our 6th of 10 in the East region came from the reports and presentations. The composite blades on our slides were a bench study on our dynamometer. They never flew.

What broke

Rotor

Printed hub and holders that did not share an axis. The eccentricity is what shook the machine in the tunnel.

Blades

Came off the shaft. One set screw into plastic is the interface every team we later benchmarked had also failed at.

Pitch

Swash plates could not hold at speed. The bevel gear box that replaced them was correct in CAD and could not be assembled in the order the parts needed.

Driveshaft

Slip ring set screws slipped, so the shaft outran the slip ring and wound the wiring around itself. Friction everywhere along the shaft.

Brake PCB

Traces melted as the board heated. It dropped the voltage but never grounded it fully.

Buck boost

An inductor that saturated too soon, switching losses, and core material that cost real efficiency.

The judges' written advice on the turbine was two lines: improve lift over drag, and fix the blade root.

03The retro blamed the process

What I was thinking: Almost every item is a process problem. Nobody wrote "the blades were wrong."

We typed up the four whiteboards from the end of season retro. Deadlines lived in nobody's calendar. Decisions were made once and never revisited. No part had a history. Sub teams did not talk to each other. The turbine was downstream of all of that.

So: a gated sequence, from requirements through two design reviews to a fall prototype and a spring one. One Notion workspace, so the club's memory stops living in my inbox. And a decision log of what we considered, dropped, and why.

Then our advisor told us the lab's fan wall is for research and the big tunnel allows one or two supervised runs a year. So the rigs we own became the iteration loop, and the rotor stand and generator dynamometer moved onto the critical path.

The physics is four lines: power, rotor speed, torque and thrust.

P = ½ ρ A Cp v³,  ω = λ v / R,  Q = P / ω,  T = ½ ρ A CT v²

With placeholder numbers until the QBlade blade is final, the rotor makes about 34 W at 10 m/s, at about 1,700 RPM.

The case nobody had written down is runaway. Lose the load, the E stop or the pitch, and the rotor speeds up until the blades make no torque. At 13 m/s that is about 4,970 RPM, close to what Cal Poly and Cal Maritime measured. Try it:

1,698 RPM rotor speed

69 N of outward pull on each blade

34.1 W of shaft power

192 N·mm of shaft torque

Under load the rotor runs at a tip speed ratio of 4. Each blade is holding back about 7 kg of pull.

What the calculator assumes

The placeholders from the blade root analysis: a 225 mm tip radius, Cp of 0.35, a tip speed ratio of 4 under load and 9 at runaway, 22 g blades with their mass centered at 99 mm, and sea level air. Above 10 m/s the controller holds the 10 m/s power. All of it changes when the QBlade blade is final.

05The root: size it for the pull

What I was thinking: Actually! the hub's lip will be load bearing... hold on let me get back to you on that.

At runaway each 22 g blade pulls outward with F = m ω² rcg = 593 N, about sixty kilograms. The bending moment at the root is only 1.22 N·m. Our year one root had never been checked against the pull. Every number here is from the eleven page analysis I wrote on Oct 2.

First I read the four public reports that describe their root joint in detail. All run 45 cm rotors, so their loads scale straight to ours.

Penn State 2026

PLA blades, root sized for bending only, centrifugal never checked. Measured 13 W at 10 m/s against 34 W predicted.

Cal Poly 2024

PLA bonded to a metal spar, ran 4,500 RPM at 22 m/s. The weak point was the plastic around the fastener. Closest to our concept.

Cal Maritime 2023

Servo and four printed miter gears that meshed poorly and could not feather at high wind.

Wisconsin 2024

Blades broke from speed and handling. Their fix was a 6 mm metal stub out of the root.

What I took from them: design for runaway. Keep mass off the tip. Let load into the plastic over an area, because the plastic around a fastener is what breaks. Give thrust, bending and pitch torque their own paths. And spin test to overspeed before the tunnel.

So the blade is printed in a tough resin with a 4 mm carbon rod bonded inside, out to about 40% span. The diameter came from σ = 32M / πd³:

RodStressSafety factorShare of root stiffnessAirfoil thickness needed
3 mm458 MPa2.236%5.0 mm
4 mm193 MPa5.264%6.0 mm
5 mm99 MPa10.182%7.0 mm
6 mm57 MPa17.591%8.0 mm

Three millimeters has no margin. Five does not fit a 7 mm thick root. The resin alone would pass a static check and fail on fatigue, since the tower shadow loads the root once per blade pass. The rod carries the fatigue.

The bonds get 25 mm into the resin where 19 would do, and 12 mm into a steel sleeve where 6 would do. From there the pull goes rod, sleeve, flange, thrust bearing, and the housing lip, the piece I had forgotten was load bearing. 06The hub: argue about it on a table

What I was thinking: I'm making a scaled up solidworks model and tryna get it printed tonight, so we can look at it in-person to analyze.

Ball bearings. Under 593 N a thrust ball bearing adds 11 N·mm to the pitch torque; a bronze bushing adds 534. Bushings would need a pitch drive 5.7 times stronger, and they stick and slip.

A bigger hub is cheap. The power lost to a hub goes as (rh/R)², so growing it from 22 to 32 mm to fit the bearings costs one percent.

Bevel gears. Three equal miter gears around one central gear collide. A 30:20 set clears by 2 mm. The analysis put 64 MPa in the teeth at the design pitch torque and called for steel; prototype one prints them in Clear V5 resin. Later I modeled one tooth in Ansys with the root fillet that the Lewis equation leaves out. The root comes out at 96 MPa with the load along the line of action, and 117 MPa under the Lewis load case, against about 60 MPa for Clear V5. The call for steel stands.

Machine the housing. The lip that carries the pull has a safety factor of 10 in aluminum and 1.9 in printed PLA.

Then the part I think mattered most. I scripted the hub, printed it at three times scale that night with color coded placeholders for every bearing and clip, and left it for the pitch sub team to take apart. Four days later the drive was rebuilt as a helix: 30 mm of stroke gives 108° of pitch at model scale.

07The actuator question nobody had run

What I was thinking: Starting up was only a problem last competition for us because we had SOOOO much friction in our driveshaft. If we make it spin as smooth as a gust of wind, I don't see cut-in speed being a problem.

The first draft carried Penn State's 22 N actuator, because theirs worked. Nobody had run the number it has to beat. A spinning blade twists toward flat pitch with ½ Jc ω² sin 2θ, which peaks at 45°, the angle a feathering blade has to pass through.

That comes to 13 to 23 N at the nut for a normal shutdown, and 90 to 160 N at a 13 m/s runaway. No version of that actuator delivers the second number. So there is a decision nothing downstream can skip:

  • Feather, and buy the force: a 200 N actuator, or a servo on a fork like a helicopter tail rotor.
  • Pitch to stall, where the twisting moment helps, and keep the light actuator, if QBlade shows stall gets the rotor below 10% RPM at every wind speed. Penn State shut down this way.

08Four bearings on one shaft is three too many

What I was thinking: Instead of it just saying, "decided to pick to use...", it could be, "We calculated that our moment on the driveshaft from the blades to be X, and the RPM to be Y, and with these bearing our driveshaft loss due to friction would be Z%."

The drivetrain is hub, shaft, generator. The generator has two bearings of its own and the shaft sits in two more. Two bearings define an axis, and printed housings cannot hold four concentric, so the shaft bends to make up the difference. A 0.1 mm offset puts 69 MPa of reversing stress in a 6 mm aluminum shaft, against a fatigue strength near 96. That is the year one misalignment, written as a stress.

So, first choice: a flexible coupling to the generator. Then one main bearing fixed and one free to slide.

Torque barely loads the shaft. Imbalance does: a gram at 99 mm makes about 27 N of rotating force at runaway. At 0.5 g the fatigue safety factor is 2.7, at 1 g it is 1.7, which is why blades get matched to a tenth of a gram.

The draft also said "full ceramic bearings" with no reason attached. The standard I asked the team to write to is the thought bubble above.

09The generator, and two questions nobody had asked

What I was thinking: I feel like Penn State would definitely flag this fly wheel as an energy storage element.

The proposed generator is an 840 g robotics outrunner. Our turbine uses about 6% of its rating, so we carry the mass and inertia of a much larger machine, and that inertia has to stop within 10 seconds. At runaway it makes 49.7 V, over the 48 V limit. And its cogging torque is unpublished, which decides whether the rotor starts at 5 m/s. The trade study has to answer all of that before we order it.

Two assumptions came with us from year one. Is a three phase generator actually required, or did we inherit it? And the flywheel: the engineering director proposed one to carry the rotor between runs. I looked up the rule. No bulk energy storage on the turbine side, and a flywheel is bulk energy storage with a shaft on it. Vetoed. Our year one cut in problem was friction.

10Reviewed, and what came back

What I was thinking: We should try and make this into comments into our CDR, resolve them, and then call that revision b.

On Oct 12 I folded the sub teams' drafts into one Critical Design Review, WP-CDR-001. Every subsystem carries a maturity label (concept, rough CAD, or proposed) so nobody mistakes a drawing for a decision. I sent it out for review on the 13th.

The comments came back on the 17th, and the headline was fair: it read like a preliminary review. Too much was still open against a Nov 17 build. Five items were marked major, and each has an answer now:

  • Shaft. Precision ground 6 mm steel instead of aluminum.
  • Bearings. Plain shielded steel. Full ceramic hates misalignment and shock, our best documented failure mode.
  • Overconstraint. A beam coupling first.
  • Feather or stall. Decided first, since everything hangs on it.
  • Set screws into prints at 5,000 RPM. No. Heat set inserts or clamps.

The printing advice went straight into the test plan: print fit coupons in the same orientation as the real parts, ream every bearing seat, and pull test the rod bond before trusting 25 mm of it. Build freeze was set for Oct 23.

Three rigs make that possible. The blade dynamometer, which I pulled a three person team together to build after year one, spins a blade to 2,500 RPM against a load cell on an ESP32, with the frame checked in ANSYS so its own shaking stays out of the numbers. The rotor test stand reads free spin RPM and thrust, so two blade designs can be compared in an afternoon. The generator dynamometer spins the generator with a drill in a pivoting cradle, its torque arm resting on a scale.

12The other half, and the program

What I was thinking: Deliverables and timelines make people show up.

Half the competition is a wind farm, designed on paper and defended in front of a different set of judges. Ours in year one was Steuben Valley Wind Farm, and it is the half that carried us. Year two gives it its own leads and its own weekly meeting, with statistics, economics and environmental students next to the mechanical majors.

Around the engineering: weekly sub team working sessions, a monthly all hands so the sub teams stay one club, and a design review at each gate. The department's parts funding arrived in October. There is still no cash sponsor.
