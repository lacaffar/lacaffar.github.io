Windpack is NC State's team for the Collegiate Wind Competition. I founded it in August 2025 with ten people and no turbine, and I am still its president. The competition scores two halves: a scaled turbine that has to perform in a wind tunnel, and a wind farm development case you defend in front of industry judges. Both halves are worth real points, and the second half is the one most people do not know exists.

We built a turbine, took it to nationals in spring 2026, and finished 6th of 10 in our first year. The turbine survived every wind tunnel run. Our composite blades beat the 3D printed baseline on measured power. We lost most of our points on things we knew were weak going in, which is the good kind of losing.

2025 / Aug

Founded the club with ten people. Split immediately into a mechanical team and an electrical team, because dividing the turbine was the only way ten people could work in parallel.

2025 / Fall

Learning year. Nobody on the team had designed a blade before. We taught ourselves QBlade, ran through a lot of airfoils and configurations, and settled on an A18 optimized for the conditions we expected.

2026 / Spring

Build and break. Structure, driveshaft, pitch, and the electrical stack all came together late enough that most of our failures showed up at once. Everything we broke, we broke early enough to fix.

2026 / May

Competition. Three days, four contests, a fifteen minute design presentation to industry judges, and a wind tunnel our turbine had never seen before.

What we built

Structure

A carbon steel base TIG welded to a carbon steel tower, with a 3D printed stanchion bolted on top. The steel is heavy on purpose: it keeps the machine from tipping with the nacelle loaded and the rotor at speed. The stanchion carries extrusions for a T slot rail, which is what everything on the driveshaft hangs from. Printing that part is what let us keep changing it.

Driveshaft

The components along the shaft added enough weight to pull it off center, so the driveshaft subteam's real job became designing 3D printed holders that mount to the T slot rail and carry the shaft. The bearing holder went through many prints before the tolerances were right: the bearing drops into a seat and a screw on top clamps it. We also added damping along the shaft to keep vibration out of the measurement.

Blades

An A18 airfoil, selected and optimized in QBlade, then modeled in SolidWorks. The approach was inspired by Cal Maritime's 2025 design. Where we broke from the field was construction: every other team ran 3D printed blades, and we laid up a composite blade with inlaid glass fiber in epoxy. It beat the printed baseline on measured power. The judges' advice was to push lift over drag further and to fix the blade root, which was the weakest joint on the machine.

Pitch system

Adaptive pitch, so the turbine can make power across a range of wind speeds. Pitching loaded blades takes more torque than we expected, so it runs a high torque motor. We started with swash plates and they could not hold the blades still at high wind speed, so we moved to a bevel gear pitch box. The pitch box and the blade attachment were designed with the blades subteam, and the whole assembly was built to come apart, which mattered more than any single design choice.

Electrical

A three phase alternator into a rectifier, an Arduino UNO control unit reading current and voltage over I2C with optocouplers protecting the controller from faults and backfeed, an electrical brake, and a buck boost stage with MPPT driving the load. The brake is a pre biased NPN transistor driving a power MOSFET: signal the transistor and the MOSFET shorts the supply, which stops power flow. Rules require the turbine to brake in extreme wind and when the load is disconnected, so that circuit is not optional.

What broke

The useful part of year one. Every one of these is a design decision we now get to make on purpose instead of by accident.

Structure

The mounting holes in the base came back threaded when they should not have been. Redrilled them. Cheap fix, but it was a drawing and specification problem, not a machining problem.

Driveshaft

Misalignment across the printed holders, and screws on the slip ring that slipped, so the shaft turned faster than the slip ring and dragged the wiring.

Blades

Blades came off their holders. The root attachment was the weak joint, and the judges said so too.

Pitch

Swash plates could not hold pitch at high wind speed. Replaced with bevel gears. Assembly was the real problem: parts that fit in CAD did not go together on a bench in the order we needed them to.

Braking PCB

Thermal management. Traces melted as the board heated and took the layout with them, and it dropped voltage more than it should have. This is the single worst reliability item on the machine.

Buck boost

Switching losses, an inductor that saturated too quickly, and core material that cost us real efficiency.

The other half

The project development contest asks for a wind farm, not a turbine: site selection, wind resource assessment, layout and energy yield, environmental and permitting review, supply chain and risk, a financial model, and a lease bid you defend. Ours was Steuben Valley Wind Farm. It ends in its own written report and its own fifteen minute presentation to a different set of judges.

There is a fourth contest most teams underrate, scored on industry partnerships, outreach, and recruiting, documented with real numbers. It is the contest where being organized is the whole skill.

Year two, what to do

This is the plan going into the 2026 to 2027 season, written down so it can be argued with. The competition moved from a single national event to regional hosting, and ours is three days at Penn State in spring 2027. We have a second year of members, a full officer board, nine subteams, and a shop we can actually get into, because I am a teaching assistant in the MAE senior design lab this year and can train members on the mills and lathes myself.

1. Run it like a program

The single biggest change from year one is cadence. A thirteen week milestone schedule from systems requirements review through shakedown, all hands on the first Tuesday of every month, weekly subteam meetings, weekly mechanical and electrical team meetings, and design reviews scheduled with faculty on both the engineering and the project development sides. Notion is the system of record: roster, tasks, milestones, purchases, meeting notes. Slack replaced the group texts. If it is not in Notion, it is not happening.

2. Get the first turbine done before Thanksgiving

A first draft turbine by November 17, and a second prototype in the spring. Year one failed mostly because everything converged at once and there was no slack left to iterate. Two prototypes with a full semester between them is the fix, and it is the reason the parts budget matters more than anything else on this list.

3. Engineering priorities, in order

  • Blade root. The judges flagged it and it failed in testing. Redesign the root joint first, before any aero work, because a better blade that comes off its holder scores nothing.
  • Braking board. Better PCB material, a heat sink, and resistors rated for the actual power. Add a mechanical brake alongside the electrical one so a thermal failure is not a total loss of braking.
  • Lift over drag. Once the root is sound, push the aero. Same QBlade workflow, more iterations, and a real test program behind it.
  • Power electronics efficiency. Attack the buck boost losses: inductor sizing so it stops saturating, better core material, and a hard look at switching losses.
  • Machined over printed. Move the parts that failed on tolerance, the bearing holders and the pitch box, off printed plastic and onto machined components. Shop access is no longer the constraint.
  • Design for assembly. Every subsystem gets an assembly order before it gets a purchase order. Year one lost days to parts that were correct in CAD and impossible on a bench.

4. Do not lose the project development half

It is worth as many points as the turbine and it is the half that starves when the build runs late. It needs its own leads, its own weekly meeting, and its own faculty design reviews, all of which it now has. Site selection, resource assessment, permitting, supply chain, and financials, staffed with statistics, electrical, and materials students alongside the mechanical majors.

5. Fix the money

Year one ran on department and student government funding plus in kind support from three companies, and nothing carries over into a new fiscal year. Every season starts at zero on parts, and there is currently no cash sponsor at all. Two prototypes need roughly two thousand dollars of materials, and the team owns no tools of its own. A new business team now sits alongside the three engineering teams to go after grants, sponsorships, and industry partnerships, which also feeds the contest that scores exactly that. If you want to help, windpack.club has the sponsorship page.
