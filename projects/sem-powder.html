The short version

Problem

Metal 3D printing powder has to be round and clean. Checking it means either a machine that only measures size, or a person counting particles in microscope images for hours.

What I built

A tool that reads SEM images, outlines every particle, finds the satellites stuck to them, and says whether two batches differ, with error bars. One command installs it on any lab PC.

Result

Particle outlines at 0.94 precision and 0.93 recall on published hand labels, above the published method's recall. Satellites are usable for before and after comparisons, not yet for absolute counts.

01The question

What I was thinking A sizing machine tells you how big the particles are and nothing about their shape. A microscope image shows the shape, but someone has to sit and count. Could the image alone do both jobs?

Metal 3D printers spread powder in thin layers and melt it with a laser or an electron beam. The powder is made by gas atomization: molten metal is blasted apart by a jet of gas and the droplets freeze in flight. Good powder comes out round and in a tight size band, roughly 15 to 53 µm for laser printers. Bad powder comes out with satellites: small particles welded onto big ones, which hurt how the powder flows and packs.

The standard check is laser diffraction. It is fast and sees millions of particles, but it assumes every particle is a sphere and reports size only. A scanning electron microscope (SEM) shows the real shapes, but an operator gets maybe 20 to 60 images in two hours and then has to measure them. The lab had about 1,000 SEM images sitting there.

03Attempt one: classic and cheap

What I was thinking Start with the simplest thing that could work, and test it where I know the right answer before I test it where I do not.

The first version ran on a laptop CPU: a classic watershed segmentation, a general purpose cell segmentation model (Cellpose 3) for comparison, a reader for the scale bar, and the standard size and shape metrics. I scored it first on synthetic powder renders where the exact answer is known (DeCost and Holm), then on real public powder images that came with laser diffraction results.

What broke

Scale bar

The first real instrument data bar it ever saw, the text reader read “100 µm” as “100 Hm”. Every image came out in pixels. Fixed by accepting any one or two letters before the m as µm, since no SEM bar says km. Scale error after the fix: 1.1%.

Too few particles

On one aluminum alloy the median size read 8% to 25% low against laser diffraction. One frame held as few as 29 whole particles and the big ones touched the edge and got dropped. Lesson written into the instructions: 5 to 10 random fields, 300 or more particles per batch.

Clumps

A cluster of satellites and fines gets measured as one big, lumpy particle (below).

04Attempt two: a foundation model on a library GPU

What I was thinking The classic method has hit its ceiling on small and touching particles. A modern segmentation model should beat it, but I will not take that on faith. Run a shoot out.

I archived attempt one and rebuilt around a GPU. Four contenders went head to head on synthetic images with exact masks plus real samples against laser data, ranked by one score fixed before looking at results:

Cellpose-SAM won, and nearly tied Segment Anything at a sixth of the time. It still found only 17% to 67% of particles under 300 pixels in area, and no setting fixed that, so fine tuning on real outlines went on the list.

Making it run where the GPU is

The GPU lives on a library workstation with no admin rights. So the whole setup became one line pasted into PowerShell: it installs Python and the CUDA build, clones the code, downloads the data and models, and runs the next stage. Rehearsing that on my laptop turned up the best bug of the project: when GPU memory ran out, the NVIDIA driver quietly spilled into system RAM instead of failing, the run crawled for ten minutes, and the page file ate 9 GB of disk. The fix caps PyTorch at 92% of GPU memory so running out is a clean error, and the next smaller training setting gets tried automatically.

05The question changed

What I was thinking The lab does not need a perfect absolute number. They need to know if the powder changed after they changed the atomizer, and how sure they can be. That is a different, and more useful, question.

The goal from the lab became quality control: compare roundness and satellites before and after an atomizer jet change. That shifted the work in three ways.

  • Train on what exists. Public hand labeled SEM powder sets (AMPIS and SALAS, 28 images and 2,248 satellite outlines) instead of waiting on labels of our own.
  • Report a difference with error bars, not a single number. Every metric gets a 95% range from resampling 1,000 times, and a verdict: changed, or no detectable change.
  • Pick the satellite model by count, not just by score. Among models within 0.02 F1 of the best, the one whose count is closest to the truth wins, because a QC report is a count.

Here is what a comparison looks like, on two public powders: plain Ti-6Al-4V against blends with 15% and 20% copper.

MetricTi-6Al-4VWith CuDifference, 95% rangeVerdict
Round (sphericity ≥ 0.90)80.5%65.4%−15.1 (−21.7 to −8.3)Changed
Carrying a satellite5.1%10.7%+5.6 (+1.7 to +9.3)Changed
Median size, by count25.5 µm19.8 µm−5.7 (−7.4 to −4.1)Changed
90th percentile size38.0 µm37.6 µm−0.4 (−2.9 to +1.9)No detectable change

The copper blends are less round and carry more satellites, while the big end of the size distribution did not move. One or two images per powder here, so the ranges come from resampling particles and are on the optimistic side. With three or more images per batch the tool resamples whole images instead.

Look at the red rings the human did not draw. Most sit on loose fines lying in the background, not stuck to anything; a few are arguable either way. Across the whole image the model rings 125 against the labeler’s 88. That disagreement is exactly why the tool leans on before and after differences: if it over-counts the same way on both batches, the change is still real.

07How good is it

Scored on the published hand labeled validation images, against the method from the paper those labels come from:

PrecisionRecallPublished (Cohn 2021)
Particles, Cellpose-SAM fine tuned0.940.930.94 / 0.80
Satellites, Mask R-CNN tuned0.670.640.69 / 0.55

Same precision on particles, with 13 points more recall. Satellites trade a little precision for 9 points of recall. Training takes about 75 minutes on the library’s 8 GB card; the trained models are a 1.4 GB public release that the installer downloads, so no other PC has to train anything. Inference runs about 5 seconds per image on a GPU.

08What I do not trust yet

  • Satellite counts are right on average across datasets but biased 15% to 20% either way depending on the powder. Trust the change between batches more than the absolute percentage, and hand count two or three of your own images once.
  • Every model was trained on public powders (nickel superalloy, aluminum, IN718, 17-4 stainless, titanium alloys), not yet on images from our own SEM.
  • An image is a 2D view of a 3D pile. Partly buried particles read small, and one frame rarely holds enough big particles for a stable 90th percentile.
  • Agreement with laser diffraction on our own batches is still unproven. That was unknown U2 on the map, and it is still open.
