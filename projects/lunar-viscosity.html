The short version

Problem

Plans to build on the Moon by melting or sintering its soil lean on viscosity data from simulants melted in Earth’s air. Iron on the Moon is in a different chemical state. No published work appears to have worked out how much that matters, soil by soil.

What I built

A pipeline trained on about 14,000 melt measurements, checked against the published lunar simulant studies, then stress tested against experiments where the oxygen was controlled. Applied to 66 real Apollo, Luna and Chang’e-5 soils.

Result

Fully molten, the difference is tiny and Earth data is fine. Near the glass transition, where sintering happens, iron rich soils soften 5 to 32 K lower on the Moon. No current model, mine included, reliably gets that direction right.

01Why the Moon, why viscosity

What I was thinking Every pound launched to the Moon is expensive. The soil is already there. If you are going to build with it by heating it, the property that runs the whole process is how easily it flows.

Lunar construction ideas that heat the soil come in two kinds. Melt processing (melt extrusion, and molten regolith electrolysis for oxygen) works with fully liquid rock. Sintering heats powder just enough that grains soften and fuse, and it is the leading method for building at scale. Both are governed by viscosity: honey is about 10 Pa·s, peanut butter about 250, and window glass is defined as solid at 1012. Molten rock spans that whole range in a few hundred degrees.

02The catch I noticed

What I was thinking Almost all of this data comes from simulant soil melted in a furnace in air. Air oxidizes iron. The Moon has essentially no oxygen. Is everyone measuring a slightly different material than the one they will actually process?

In the 2025 Stapperfend simulant studies, about 60% of the iron is in the oxidized Fe3+ form after melting in air. Lunar soil formed below the iron-wüstite buffer (written IW−1 below), where nearly all iron is Fe2+, about 2% Fe3+. Iron’s state changes how the silicate network links up, which changes viscosity. So: for each returned soil, how much do the key processing temperatures shift between an Earth test and the Moon, and where are current models too uncertain to say?

03Aim where it matters

What I was thinking If the shift is small when fully molten and large near the glass transition, then the interesting part is sintering. Aim there, and design the test before the model.

The plan, written down before any training:

  • Train on 13,981 water free melt measurements from the open gpvisc database.
  • Hold out 16 lunar simulant melts from the two 2025 Stapperfend papers. None is within 5.9 cation percent of anything in training.
  • Stress test on experiments where oxygen was controlled and the iron state was measured by titration.
  • Apply to 66 returned soils with full chemistry.
  • Model: about 12 oxide fractions in, with iron split into FeO and Fe2O3 by a redox model at air or at IW−1. Out come the three parameters of the standard Vogel-Fulcher-Tammann equation, log η = A + B / (T − C), so temperature only ever enters through the physics. A deep ensemble of ten networks gives the uncertainty.

The architecture is not new, and I said so in the brief. The question is: nobody appears to have computed uncertainty mapped Earth vs Moon windows before.

04First, check my own brief

What I was thinking My brief has numbers in it I got from reading. Before building on them, check them against the raw sources.

Three of them did not survive:

“60% Fe³⁺”

Right number, but it is calculated from a redox model, not measured. Both sides of the Earth vs Moon comparison are model outputs, and the write up says so.

Reduced iron data

I had read that only 50 measurements have lunar-like reduced iron. The database actually has 375, across 10 studies. Same conclusion, stronger form: only one composition has reduced iron near the glass transition.

A paper’s table

Two simulants in a published table carry misprinted fit parameters. They miss the paper’s own data by 0.7 to 1.1 orders of magnitude. Refitting reproduces the printed glass transition to within 1 K.

07The stress test, the key result

What I was thinking A model can score well overall and still be flat, or backwards, along the one axis I care about. So test that axis directly, against experiments where the iron state was measured, not assumed.

Seven controlled oxygen series from Chevrel’s open access thesis, none of them in my training data. Before you look at the answer, make the call yourself.

And the ones that get the direction right still miss the size.

  • Fully molten, the measured effect is at most 0.14 log units. Every machine learning model, gpvisc included, overstates it 3 to 10 times.
  • Near the glass transition, gpvisc has the sign wrong in 5 of 7 series, even though it was trained on those studies.
  • Sehlke and Whittington, run the way the 2025 study ran it, predicts the opposite of what was measured in 12 of 13 series. My reproduction traces that to how its spreadsheet books iron oxide. In the paper’s own convention the model cannot see redox at all.

A likely cause, consistent with what I found in the database: most training values for Fe3+ were calculated from temperature at air, not measured, so redox is tangled up with temperature and composition instead of being learned.

09The bottleneck is data, not models

What I was thinking Every road ends at the same gap. The most useful output of this project might be the experiment someone should run next.

In every source I found, no basaltic melt has a measured viscosity or glass transition below about 12% Fe3+. The Moon is near 2%. Whether the glass transition keeps falling below 12% or levels off is worth a factor of about 4 in the predicted shift. That is the bracket in the chart above.

Ranking candidate experiments by how much the ensemble disagrees with itself points at the same gap: every top slot is near the glass transition, and all but one is at lunar oxygen with 10% or more FeO. Chang’e-5 soil ranks first. The practical version: melt the high titanium simulant OPRL2NT under CO and CO2 at IW−1, and measure its glass transition and viscosity below 10% Fe3+.

Limits

  • The shift is extrapolated: measured series span 12% to 84% Fe3+, and the Moon is near 2%. The bracket covers this, but it is an assumption.
  • The Earth reference matters as much as model choice. A glass melted at 1500°C and a powder sintered at 1100°C start from different iron states; a sintering test should report its sample’s Fe3+.
  • Metallic iron (about 0.5% in mature soils) and crystals are ignored. Real sintering is of partly crystalline powder, not glass.
  • The ensemble’s error bars are honest on familiar chemistry (93% coverage) but overconfident on the simulants (76%), which are alkali rich and out of distribution.

Still to do
