SEM Powder QC

Teaching a computer to grade metal 3D printing powder from microscope photos, and to say when a batch really changed. Undergraduate research since Sept 2026 at CAMAL, NC State’s Center for Additive Manufacturing and Logistics.

Code on GitHub ↗

01Background

Metal 3D printers melt thin layers of powder. At CAMAL I help make it by gas atomization, which really just means spraying molten metal. Melt the metal at the top, pour it through a nozzle, and blast it with gas so it breaks into droplets that freeze in flight and fall to the bottom as powder. Good ones freeze round. Bad ones grow satellites, small particles welded onto big ones that make the powder flow worse.

To check a batch, labs use laser diffraction: shine a laser through the powder and read how the particles scatter it. It is by far the fastest way to size powder. But it only gives numbers, how big the particles are and how spread out. It can’t tell a round particle from one covered in satellites, so it can’t judge the quality.

A scanning electron microscope (SEM) image does show shape, but someone has to count the particles by hand. The lab had about 1,000 SEM images and no fast way to measure them.

03Attempt one: machine learning? no thank you!

What I was thinking: Start with the simplest thing that could work.

My first version was a feature recognition program, so no actual machine learning. A textbook method called watershed floods the image’s brightness like a landscape, and the lines where the pools meet become particle edges.

  • Close on size: within 8% of the lab’s laser instrument
  • Fast: a second per image on a laptop
  • Bad at satellites: 0.48 out of 1

Its worst habit: measuring a clump as one big particle.

04Attempt two: spending a very long night trying to find my new direction

What I was thinking: The classic method has hit its ceiling on small and touching particles. A modern segmentation model should beat it, but I will not take that on faith. Run a shoot out.

So I spent one long, long night at the library on a computer with a strong graphics card (GPU), racing four ways of outlining particles just to find a direction:

05Then the question changed

What I was thinking: The lab doesn’t need perfect numbers. They need to know if the powder changed after an atomizer change, and how sure to be.

So I trained on public hand labeled images and made every difference come with a 95% range. A test on a titanium alloy powder, Ti-6Al-4V, against blends with copper:

MetricTi-6Al-4V → with CuVerdict
Round (sphericity ≥ 0.90)80.5% → 65.4%−15.1 points, 95% range −21.7 to −8.3Changed
Carrying a satellite5.1% → 10.7%+5.6 points, 95% range +1.7 to +9.3Changed
90th percentile size38.0 → 37.6 µm−0.4, 95% range −2.9 to +1.9No detectable change

Copper made the powder less round, with twice the satellites.

06What is inside the tool

Two models run side by side, one for whole particles and one for satellites, and plain geometry joins them.

  • A vision transformer cuts the image into small patches and lets every patch compare itself with every other one. Cellpose-SAM borrows one from Segment Anything, which Meta trained on millions of photos.
  • A convolutional network like ResNet-50 slides small learned filters over the image, from edges up to shapes. Mask R-CNN uses one to find satellites and score each from 0 to 1.
  • Neither knew powder at first, so I fine tuned both: trained them further on public hand labeled SEM images.

07How good is it?

Scored on published hand labeled images, against the published method:

  • 93% of particles found, against 80%, and both are right 94% of the time about what they outline
  • 64% of satellites found, against 55%
  • About 5 seconds per image

08Limits, and what is next

Do not trust yet

  • Satellite counts run 15% to 20% off depending on the powder, so trust the change between batches more than the count.
  • All training was on public powders, not yet on our own.

Next

  • Run it on the lab’s own images and hand check a few.
  • Fine tune on our own powder; the labeling tool is ready.
