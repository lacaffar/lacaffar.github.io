Building with the Moon’s dirt

Launching mass to the Moon is the expensive part, so the case for building with lunar soil is simple: the feedstock is already there. The hard part is everything between a scoop of dirt and a part you would trust to hold something up.

This is my map of that chain. I came at it from two ends this semester: how you check powder quality from images (SEM Powder QC), and how the Moon’s chemistry moves the temperatures you process regolith at (Lunar Regolith Viscosity). The rest is me thinking out loud, so treat it as notes, not a design.

What is different up there

Vacuum

No air means no convection. A hot part loses heat only by radiation, which is fast when it is glowing and slow once it cools. Anything printed from melt cools on a very different curve than it would on Earth.

No oxygen

Iron stays in its reduced Fe2+ form. Most melt data comes from simulants melted in air, where about 60% of the iron is oxidized. Near the glass transition that moves processing temperatures by 5 to 32 K for iron rich soils.

Dust

Regolith is fine, sharp and abrasive. It gets into seals, bearings and anything that slides, and there is no rain to wash it off.

Long days

Away from the poles, daylight and darkness each last about two Earth weeks. Power and thermal design have to survive both.

Distance

A signal takes about 2.6 seconds to get to the Moon and back. You can steer from Earth, slowly, but nobody is walking over to clear a jam.

Site matters

Mare soils average about 17% FeO, highland soils about 6%. Across 66 returned soils that is well over 100 degrees difference in the temperature to reach the same viscosity. Where you land decides how hot you have to run.

The chain, stage by stage

Six stages. For each one, what sets the requirement, what a first factory must have, and what is nice to have once the first one works. Flip the switch to see the bare minimum.

must havenice to have

1Dig

Set by: dust, power, and knowing which soil you are standing on.

  • An excavator built for abrasive dust: sealed joints, few sliding surfaces.
  • A site whose soil chemistry is already mapped. Mare or highland changes every stage after this one.
  • Digging and hauling on its own through the two week night.
  • Selecting one soil type on purpose instead of taking whatever is under the scoop.

2Sort

Set by: what particle sizes the heating and forming steps can handle.

  • Sieving to a size band, and throwing out rocks.
  • Magnetic separation, to pull out grains rich in metallic iron.
  • Blending minerals on purpose to land the mix in a target viscosity window. This is the stretch goal on my viscosity project, not started yet.

3Check the feedstock

Set by: the heating step only works inside a window, and the window depends on what went in.

  • Size and shape of every batch, from a camera and a model rather than a lab. This is the SEM Powder QC idea moved to the Moon: images in, size distribution and shape out.
  • Bulk chemistry, iron above all, since it sets processing temperatures.
  • Batch to batch drift with error bars, the same before and after comparison the SEM tool reports.
  • Measuring the iron oxidation state directly instead of assuming it.

4Heat

Set by: viscosity. Sintering lives between softening and the glass transition; melt processing lives above the working point.

  • Process windows calibrated to lunar iron, not air melted simulants. Fully molten, the difference is small. Near the glass transition, where sintering happens, it is 5 to 32 K for iron rich soils, and no current model predicts it reliably.
  • Temperature control tighter than the window. For a mare soil near the glass transition, every 10 K is roughly half an order of magnitude in viscosity.
  • The lowest peak temperature that works. Less energy, a wider choice of furnace materials, and less wear on the hot zone.
  • Sharing heat with oxygen extraction, since molten regolith electrolysis needs the same melt anyway.
  • Concentrated sunlight as the heat source during the day.

5Shape

Set by: how fast the material stops flowing once it is placed.

  • A forming method matched to the regime. For melt extrusion, the melt goes down near the working point (103 Pa·s) and has to reach softening (106.6), where it stops sagging under its own weight, before the next layer.
  • A choice between soils that suits the process. Mare melts need less heat; highland melts freeze faster in vacuum. Neither is free.
  • Building large parts in place, so nothing has to be carried after it is made.

6Inspect

Set by: nobody is going to destructively test every part.

  • A way to call a part good without a lab: dimensions from a scan, density from weight and volume.
  • Watching for defects while the part is being made, the way CAMAL is working toward for electron beam printing on Earth.
  • Melting rejected parts back into feedstock.

+Across every stage

Set by: the Moon.

  • Power that survives the night.
  • Recovering from a jam or a fault without a person there.
  • Keeping dust out of every moving part.
  • Parts a robot can swap.

Where my two projects plug in

Stage 3, check the feedstock. The SEM tool already does the thing a lunar line needs: it turns images into size, shape and a verdict on whether a batch changed, with error bars, without a sizing instrument. Its lesson that transfers: trust the change between batches more than the absolute number.

Stage 4, heat, and the choice of site. The viscosity work says the Earth data is fine for melting and not fine for sintering, and that the gap is a missing measurement, not a missing model. Anyone sintering mare soil should want near-glass transition viscosity data on reduced iron before trusting a temperature.

What I do not know yet

  • How much the atmosphere inside a sealed furnace on the Moon would differ from the vacuum outside it, and so which iron state the material actually sees.
  • How partly crystalline powder sinters compared with the glass my model assumes.
  • What a kilogram of sintered block costs in energy, and whether that, not viscosity, is what decides between sintering and melting.
  • Which of the must haves above someone has already solved and I have not read yet. Probably several.
