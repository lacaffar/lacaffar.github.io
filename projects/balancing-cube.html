5 min read, plus a cube you can try to balance

Balancing Cube

A 165 mm cube built to stand on one corner, held there by three flywheels spinning inside it.

The short version

The idea

Reproduce the Cubli from ETH Zurich from scratch. My own CAD, circuit board and code, nothing copied from theirs.

The hard part

Tight tolerances on a budget. 3D printed parts never come out exactly the size I drew, and everything has to fit inside 165 mm.

What I built

A shell that checks its own fit, faces with an inlaid drawing, and my own circuit board.

01Simulate it before buying anything

Before I ordered a single part, I wrote a physics model of the cube balancing on one edge, and a controller to hold it up, in Python with NumPy and SciPy. The model is a state space system in tilt, tilt rate and wheel speed, linearized about the balance point, with the motor modeled from its measured back EMF and stall torque. SciPy solves the Riccati equation for the LQR gains, and those gains go straight into the firmware.

The model also ties the mechanical side to the electrical side. The cube stays up only while the motor’s torque beats gravity pulling on the center of gravity. With the motor I bought, that sets the steepest lean it can ever hold, about 6°. So where the center of gravity sits matters: 20 mm higher, and the limit drops to 5.1°.

The game below runs the same model in JavaScript, with full gravity instead of the linear version. Hold a button or the arrow keys to spin the wheel and push the cube back up. Start in slow motion.

The Riccati equation

ATP + PA − PB R−1BTP + Q = 0

K = R−1BTP

P is what SciPy solves for. K is the gain on tilt, tilt rate and wheel speed: 497 V per radian of tilt.

Center of gravity against the motor

M g l sin θ ≤ τstall = Kt (12 V) / R

θmax = sin−1 (0.12 / (1.0 · 9.81 · 0.117)) = 6.0°

l is the height of the center of gravity above the edge, 117 mm. The motor gives 0.12 N·m at stall on 12 V. The lean is drawn bigger than 6° so it shows.

At real speed a one degree lean becomes a thirty degree fall in about half a second, faster than anyone can react. And push one way for too long and the wheel runs out of speed to give.

Then I rebuilt it in MATLAB and Simulink with the motor numbers I measured. MATLAB’s lqr gives the same gains as my SciPy script. The Simulink model runs the full nonlinear cube, samples the controller at 500 Hz like the ESP32 loop, and clips the motor at the 12 V supply. It catches a 3° lean in about a third of a second, and a push at 1.5 s peaks at half a degree with the motor pinned at 12 V.

The fine print

It is a one wheel model of this cube on an edge: 165 mm, about 1 kg, a 128 mm PLA wheel with its bolts, and the RS-385 as I measured it, stalling at 0.12 N·m with a back EMF of 0.0186 V·s/rad, so about 6,160 rpm free at 12 V. With those numbers it can only hold itself up from about 5 degrees of tilt.

The demo controller is the same LQR as the Simulink model, and it can see the wheel’s speed. The RS-385s have no encoders, so on the cube that takes a hall sensor and a magnet per wheel, about $1 each.

02Order the cheap parts, then put them on one board

The first order was about $23: three RS-385 motors, five TB6612FNG motor drivers so there are spares, and an MPU-6050 gyro. An ESP32 runs it and a 3S lithium polymer battery powers it. All of it plugs into one board, Reaction Cube Carrier v1.

04A shell that checks its own fit

3D printed parts never come out exactly the size I drew, and the motor listings disagree on length. So the shell is a Python script: a dozen numbers in, every part out, each one checked against every other. Caliper the real parts, change a number, rerun it.

05Is a 450 mAh battery enough?

Yes, for now. The pack is 3S, 450 mAh, about 5 Wh. Balancing on a corner should draw 0.5 to 1 A, so 20 to 40 minutes, plenty for tuning in short bursts. It can also push 36 A into a short, which is why a 5 A fuse sits right after it.

06Where it stands, Nov 23 2026

The first time I powered it up, nothing worked, and I was sure my board was the problem. It wasn’t. I had soldered the header pins only on the carrier side. On top, where they come up through the driver boards under the black plastic strips, they weren’t soldered at all (left).

So I pulled the strips off and soldered the tops (right). The three green wires came later. After I had already ordered the board, I decided I wanted hall sensors to measure each wheel’s speed, and the board had no place for them. The header I had added for an LED strip now powers them, and their signal wires go straight to the ESP32.

For the first test runs the motors were clamped to the bench with the wheels off. Mounting them perfectly level is still the hard part: the way I clamp them isn’t good enough yet.

And one of the motor drivers blew up, violently. I think I did it by using the motors as speakers, reversing them 1,500 times a second. It’s the red board with the chip burned black, inside the shell:
